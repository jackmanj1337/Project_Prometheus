Typing on a phone, when the controls already own half the screen

Every number here is measured against the shipped grid layout and the ratified Compact budget — 360×640 logical pixels, a control band that is never covered, and 240px of content above it. Nothing is drawn to taste.

Decided — 2026-08-06 Suppress the OS keyboard. During a text-entry session our keyboard takes over the control band. Nothing is covered, nothing floats, and the 288px the controller occupies is the same 288px a keyboard needs. The three questions below are what that decision leaves open.

The budget it has to live inWhy the band was always the only place to put it

The control band is 45% of a 360×640 screen. Add an OS keyboard at a typical 40–50% of viewport height and the field being edited has 5–15% of the screen left. That is the arithmetic that rules the OS keyboard out, not a preference.

ResearchHow does an OS keyboard get away with keys this small?

Worth answering before picking a layout, because if the trick transfers, ten columns is back on the table. It does not transfer — and the reason is specific to what this game asks people to type.

Start with the number nobody advertises. Google’s own decoder paper puts raw touchscreen text entry at 8–9% per-letter error, and says plainly why you never notice: “autocorrection … work[s] in the background, silently correcting our typos and misspellings.” The small keys are not accurate. The software behind them is.

Four things stacked on top of each other

What is actually holding the accuracy up
MechanismWhat it doesTransfers to us?
Hit area ≠ drawn key The gaps between keys belong to their neighbours. There is no dead space anywhere on the surface. Yes — free
Spatial model A tap is not hit-tested. It becomes “a Gaussian distribution centered on each key center” — a probability over every key, not one answer. Yes — but useless alone
Language model Bayes over whole sequences: tap “q”, then the u/i boundary, and “qu” wins. Measured at 1.67–1.87× fewer errors. No — see below
Key-target resizing The invisible target of a likely-next key silently grows. Needs “anchoring”, because aggressive resizing stops people typing what they meant. No — same reason

A fifth finding reframes the whole problem: Holz and Baudisch showed that 67% of touch inaccuracy is a systematic offset — per user, per finger posture — rather than the “fat finger” blur everyone assumes. Systematic means correctable. That is the headroom the industry has been spending for fifteen years.

Why the language model is the one thing we cannot borrow

Almost everything a player types here is a proper noun — a save name, a profile name, a campaign or unit name. Those are out-of-vocabulary by definition, and OOV is precisely where the decoder stops helping and starts fighting.

Gboard keeps an entire parallel literal decoding path specifically so out-of-vocabulary words can be typed at all, and its authors call balancing that against autocorrection “tricky”. The key-target resizing work names the same hazard from the other end: resize too eagerly and you “violate basic user expectations” by making the intended text untypeable.

So the mechanism that pays for small keys is the mechanism that is worst at the one job we need. Naming your save Ragnell is the adversarial case, not the happy path.

Measured against a real iPhone keyboard

On a 1179×2556 phone at 460 ppi the screen is 65.1 mm across, so one logical pixel is 0.166 mm. That converts every option into millimetres of glass and makes the comparison direct.

Two things fall out. First, Apple does not follow its own 44pt rule on its own keyboard — the keys meet it vertically and miss it badly horizontally, because rows are unambiguous and columns are what the language model disambiguates. Second, the option this document already recommended is not a cautious one: B’s keys are physically bigger than an iPhone key in both dimensions. That is the argument for it, rather than a guideline citation.

Option C is what copying Apple honestly looks like — narrow, tall, 26 letters in three rows of ten plus a function row, and it fits the band with 106px to spare. It is listed because it is the shape the research points at, and rejected because it lands below the size Apple already needs a decoder to rescue.

What we can take, for free

Two of the four mechanisms cost nothing and are worth taking whichever layout wins. Claim the gaps: draw 44px keys but hit-test 48px, so the 4px gutters belong to their neighbours instead of being dead space — the grid presenter currently leaves them dead. Correct the systematic offset: people hit low, and Holz and Baudisch showed that is a fixed bias rather than noise, so a small constant upward nudge on the hit test helps everyone and costs one line.

And there is one place a lexicon genuinely does apply: the active campaign pack is an enumerable vocabulary. Unit names, class names and item names are all known. For fields that name one of those, a filtered candidate list beats a keyboard outright — that is a real lexicon, not a language model guessing at free text.

Question 1 — the layoutKeep ten columns, or reflow to seven?

The shipped layout (us_ascii_grid.json, ABC layer) is 7 rows × 10 columns. Width is the binding constraint, not height. At 360 logical px with 8px gutters there are 344px to spend; at the 44px touch minimum that is 7 columns, and no more.

This question got sharper the moment the band became the keyboard. The 10-column layout used to be fine because it was navigated with a d‑pad — but the d‑pad is exactly what the keyboard replaced. There is nothing left to navigate it with.

Question 3 — the settingWhat happens to “system” in the Text Entry menu?

system is in VALID_MODES but no backend is ever registered, and TextEntryRegistry.resolve() falls back to hardware for an unregistered mode. hardware only consumes physical key events. So on a phone, picking it today gives you a field you cannot type into at all.

Before this decision that was a stub waiting for a backend. After it, there is no backend coming — which changes the answer.

One thing to get right, whichever way the three go

The OS keyboard is not currently suppressed, and one line of existing code invites it. TextEntryService.gd:60 calls _target.grab_focus() on a real LineEdit, and virtual_keyboard_enabled — which defaults to true — is set nowhere in the repo.

On any platform whose DisplayServer implements the virtual keyboard, focusing that field raises the OS keyboard on top of ours. Two keyboards, and the one the player did not ask for is in front. Whether Godot 4.6.3’s web DisplayServer implements it is not verified and should be measured on a real export rather than recalled — the existing Playwright recipe answers it in one run.

Landscape is fine, for the record: at 852×393 the band is 177px, which takes three 44px rows — enough for 26 letters at 13 wide plus a function row. It needs its own arrangement, not a different answer.

What is still yours to call

  1. Question 1 — layout. B, and the research strengthens it rather than merely confirming it: B’s keys are physically larger than an iPhone key, while A’s are smaller than one — without the decoder that makes an iPhone key work, on exactly the proper nouns where a decoder would not help.
  2. Two free wins, whatever else is decided: hit-test the 4px gaps instead of leaving them dead, and apply a constant upward offset to the hit point. Both are borrowed straight from how OS keyboards work and neither needs a language model.
  3. Question 2 — the echo strip. I would take B. The 52px is already there, and A leaves the player typing into a field they cannot see on the exact screens where text entry happens most.
  4. Question 3 — the setting. I would take A. B spends a quarter of a Compact screen advertising something that is now decided against, not merely unbuilt.
  5. A consequence to accept explicitly: the touch density tokens do not survive the keyboard intact — gap 8 → 4, gutter 16 → 8. Either the keyboard is a named exception to the token set, or the tokens gain a compact variant. It should not be an undocumented local override.
